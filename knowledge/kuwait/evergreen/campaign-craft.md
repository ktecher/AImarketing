# Campaign craft

How campaigns are known to work, from outside this shop. The Kuwait specifics sit
in the files beside this one; this is the part that would be true in any market,
and is still the part most often got wrong — including by a model, which has read
a decade of agency blog posts and will reproduce their advice unless told
otherwise.

**Everything from the first section heading to the provenance section is written
without a single digit, on purpose.** `craft()` in `lib/meeting.ts` pastes that
half into the planner's prompt, where CLAUDE.md rule 2 forbids the model emitting
a number at all. A prompt that hands it "sixty-forty" is a prompt teaching it to
write figures. The evidence, with its figures and its caveats, sits below that
fence, where only a person reads it.

## More buyers, not fonder buyers

Campaigns recruiting people who do not buy from us yet beat campaigns aimed at
existing customers, on business results rather than sentiment. Most buyers in any
category buy rarely, and a campaign written for the enthusiast reaches the people
who were coming in anyway. When the answer to *who is this for* is "our
customers", ask who is **not** buying from us yet.

## The widest sensible audience, the narrowest possible message

Tight targeting reads as efficiency and behaves as a ceiling — the most effective
campaigns talk to the whole category. Narrow the message, not the audience.
Narrowing the audience is what a planner does when it cannot decide what to say.

## Buying situations, not brackets

People retrieve a shop by the situation they are in — why, when, where, with
whom, with what, feeling what, while doing what. A campaign that shows the
situation is remembered when the situation comes round again.

"Students starting the year, and the parents actually paying" is a situation.
"Ages eighteen to twenty-four" is a bracket and tells nobody what to write. Ask
which moment the purchase happens in: the laptop that died the week before term,
the phone that will not last the shift, the gift that has to be bought today.

A shop grows by being the obvious answer in **more** situations, not by being
loved harder in one.

## Building and selling are different jobs

Activation — the offer, the deadline, the price — moves stock now and stops the
day it stops. Brand-building works slowly and is what makes activation cheap.
Across a year brand-building is the larger share of the money; inside one short
retail burst nearly everything is activation, which is why the planner should
argue for keeping something back for the work that outlives the week. Even a
clearance can afford a share for production and creative — it still teaches
people what this shop is.

A promotion with nothing behind it is a discount, not a campaign.

## Fame and feeling beat argument

Campaigns that got talked about produced the strongest business results, and work
that made people feel something beat work that argued a case. Specs are the
reason to believe, not the idea; a spec sheet is a leaflet.

## A campaign is a window, not a day

Effects accumulate with the length of the run. Say when it starts, roughly how
long it runs, and what the second half does that the first did not.

## Being heard from

Brands heard from more than their size would predict tend to grow; brands heard
from less tend to shrink. On a small budget that argues for concentration:
noticed on one channel beats invisible on four.

## Recognition is built by looking the same

Same logo, colours, typeface and framing, every piece, every week. Work that
reinvents itself each time buys recognition for nobody — that is what the
template studio here is for.

## What none of this licenses

None of it is a fact about Kuwait, this shop or any product. It says nothing
about what a channel reaches here, what anything costs, or whether an idea will
work. The figures come from the person and the catalogue, and a claim that is not
in a retrieved row is not said at all.

## Provenance

**Recruitment, reach, emotion, fame, campaign length and share of voice** — Les
Binet and Peter Field, *The Long and the Short of It* (IPA, 2013), analysing the
IPA Databank of 996 campaigns entered into the IPA Effectiveness Awards,
1980–2010. Read as summarised by Alex Murrell, verified 2026-09-07:
https://www.alexmurrell.co.uk/summaries/les-binet-and-peter-field-the-long-and-the-short-of-it

- The brand-building / sales-activation split is **60:40** in that data — the
  ratio that maximised profit gain across the databank.
- Loyalty campaigns produced roughly **a third** of the business effects of
  acquisition campaigns.
- Emotional campaigns were **almost twice** as likely to produce top-box profit
  growth over the long term.
- Share of voice above share of market predicts growth; below it, decline.

*Read it with its limits in view.* These are entries to an effectiveness award,
submitted by agencies with something to prove, overwhelmingly UK, and the newest
of them is from 2010 — before Snapchat existed. The direction of each finding has
held up in later work; none of the figures is a constant, and 60:40 in particular
is a long-run average across categories, not a rule for a two-week electronics
promotion. It is quoted here as the argument for keeping some budget off the
offer, never as an allocation to obey.

**Buying situations and the seven Ws** (why, when, where, with whom, with what,
feeling what, while doing what) — Jenni Romaniuk and Byron Sharp, *How Brands
Grow Part 2* (2015), Ehrenberg-Bass Institute; mental availability from Byron
Sharp, *How Brands Grow* (2010). Read at a secondary source, verified 2026-09-07:
https://www.selfstorming.com/tools/libraries/marketing-laws/category-entry-points

*That page is a marketing-AI vendor's own library*, not the institute, and it is
cited only for the framework's shape, which matches the books. Nothing
quantitative was taken from it. The institute's own material is largely behind
sponsorship: https://marketingscience.info

**Not taken from anywhere.** Meta's and Snapchat's official creative-guidance
